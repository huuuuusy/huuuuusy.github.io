<h2>Shiyu Hu <span class="intro-name-zh" lang="zh-CN">(胡世宇)</span></h2>
</div>
</div>
<p>AI is moving from single questions to sustained tasks in open environments. Unmanned systems must keep perceiving as light and motion change, multimodal large models must follow ever longer videos, and LLM-based agents must act as people over long interactions. In such tasks, getting the current step right is not enough; what a system carries forward from earlier steps must still hold. A tracker may lose a target that leaves the view and returns, a video model may answer correctly while its opening description of the scene has gone stale, and a virtual student may sound like a learner without behaving like one.</p>
<p>So my question is simple: <strong>when an AI gets it right, what has it actually kept?</strong> A correct answer at one moment does not show it; to find out, something has to change. My research studies <strong>the dynamic states of humans and AI in open environments</strong>: what a system should keep, update, or leave open as new observations arrive. I design tasks and benchmarks that let conditions change, let time pass, and bring people in, with human performance and judgment as the reference where no single ground truth exists. I also build models that hold these states better, and test them in open-environment visual perception, long and streaming video understanding, and human–AI education.</p>
